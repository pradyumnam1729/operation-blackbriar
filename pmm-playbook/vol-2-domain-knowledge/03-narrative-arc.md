# 03 — The 7-Step Narrative Arc

*Source: Talya Heller G. Encoded from Master Instructions §7.2.*

---

## When This Standard Applies

The narrative arc applies to **any buyer-facing narrative**: sales decks, one-pagers, launch stories, deal narratives, exec briefs with an external audience. It is the structural skeleton under the copy — the order in which a buyer is walked from their status quo to conviction. All activation agents producing buyer-facing narrative apply it (B6, B7, B8 especially), and the asset templates in Vol 9 are built on it.

The arc's center of gravity is the buyer, not the product. The product does not appear until step 6 of 7. If a draft mentions the product in its first third, it has broken the arc.

---

## The Seven Steps

| Step | What it says | Do | Don't |
|------|--------------|----|----|
| **1. The old way** | "That's how we do it" | Quantifiable status quo the buyer sees themselves in — their process, their numbers, their words | Generic, vague, or nonexistent status quo |
| **2. What changed** | "That's why it's been harder" | Specific shift, tied to their metrics (regulation, market pressure, scale) | Generic "AI is changing everything" hand-waving |
| **3. The tension** | "We're stuck between…" | Name the forced tradeoff they can feel (speed vs. consistency, coverage vs. depth) | Jumping straight from problem to solution |
| **4. Cost of inaction** | "This costs us every month" | Quantified, named risks and stakes — money, time, standing | No urgency; a costless problem enables the status quo |
| **5. Why alternatives aren't enough** | "The usual fixes won't work" | A maturity model with clear ceilings: each alternative helps until it structurally stops | Comparison tables or trash-talk |
| **6. What success looks like** | "Here's what is possible" | The capability stack — what becomes possible in their world | Feature dump, screenshot parade |
| **7. Proof you can deliver** | "Here's who this works for and why" | Before/after, proof placed near claims, objections predicted and answered | Logo wall plus a single case study |

Notes on the two most-fumbled steps:

- **Step 5 is a maturity model, not a takedown.** "A shared drive solves storage; it cannot solve freshness or framing" respects the buyer's past choices while showing their ceiling. A comparison table dunking on competitors triggers defensiveness and invites a feature-by-feature rebuttal. (This is also A3's quality gate: intelligence feeds ceilings, not insults.)
- **Step 7 predicts objections.** Proof is not decoration at the end; each proof point sits next to the claim it supports, and the step anticipates what the buying committee will ask when we are not in the room — which leads directly to the leave-behind principle.

---

## The Champion Leave-Behind Principle

> The deck is a leave-behind that arms the champion to sell internally to a buying committee that wasn't in the demo.

The person we present to is rarely the person who signs. After the meeting, our champion re-presents our story to a CFO, a CIO, a procurement officer — without us. The narrative must therefore work *when we are not there to narrate it*:

- **Self-contained.** Every slide or section survives out of context; no claim depends on the talk track.
- **Committee-ready.** It helps the champion defend the four things committees attack: budget, switching cost, implementation effort, and training burden. If the leave-behind is silent on these, the champion improvises or folds.
- **In their language.** The champion will paraphrase; give them phrasing worth stealing — which is why raw customer language from `our-customer.md` matters even in structure-heavy assets.

"It's about them, not you" is the arc's one-line summary and the test for every draft.

## Worked Miniature (PMM Agent, sold to a CMO)

1. *Old way:* one PMM, a ticket queue, 3-day median turnaround on asset requests.
2. *What changed:* portfolio went from one product to four; the field doubled; asks tripled.
3. *Tension:* answer fast and lose consistency, or stay consistent and become the bottleneck.
4. *Cost of inaction:* every week, reps run deals on stale or improvised decks; two versions of the story reached the same buyer last quarter.
5. *Alternatives' ceilings:* more PMM hires add capacity, not consistency; a content repository stores, it doesn't answer; generic AI answers, but from nothing.
6. *Success:* every role gets cited, role-framed answers in a minute; every asset traces to one approved foundation.
7. *Proof:* our own asset log — turnaround medians, usage rates, consistency scores from [Vol 1, ch. 06](../vol-1-product/06-success-metrics.md).

## Enforcement

- Asset templates for decks, one-pagers, and launch stories (Vol 9) are structured as the seven steps; a generated draft carries the arc by construction.
- The asset-qa gate checks arc integrity: product-first openings, missing cost-of-inaction, and step-5 trash-talk are rejection reasons.
- The Voice of Aurigo cardinal rule — open from the reader's world — is step 1 of the arc; the two standards enforce each other.
