# 02 — Problem Statement

---

## The Fragmentation Problem

Product marketing knowledge at a typical B2B software company is not missing. It is fragmented — spread across decks, folders, inboxes, call recordings, and one overloaded person's memory. The Master Instructions (§1) describe the state precisely: a product update lands in one system, customer feedback sits in another, sales needs support for a live opportunity, and the existing assets are quietly going stale. Nothing connects the signals to coordinated action.

The result is not the absence of messaging. It is worse: the presence of five versions of it.

---

## The Five Failure Modes

### 1. The ticket queue to PMM

Every question about positioning, competitors, or proof points becomes a request to one person. "Can you send me the latest Primus deck?" "What do we say when they bring up Oracle?" "Do we have a case study for water utilities?" Each request is small; the queue is not. The PMM's calendar becomes the company's messaging API, with a latency measured in days and a throughput of one.

The queue also inverts priorities. Master Instructions §3.5 budgets ad hoc work at ~10% of PMM time. In a fragmented state, ad hoc requests consume 40–60% because every request is urgent to its requester and there is no system to absorb it. Sand fills the jar before the rocks go in.

### 2. Stale decks in circulation

Assets do not announce their expiry. The battlecard written in 2025-03 still circulates in 2026-08, carrying pricing that changed twice, a competitive claim the competitor has since neutralized, and a customer logo that churned. Nobody is lying; the deck is just old, and nothing flags it. Stale assets are more dangerous than missing ones — a rep with no battlecard asks for help; a rep with a stale battlecard walks into the landmine confidently.

### 3. Folder archaeology

The knowledge exists somewhere: a shared drive, a wiki, an old launch folder, an email thread. Finding it is an archaeology project. Sales reps report spending real selling time hunting for assets, then give up and use whatever was already on their laptop. The cost is invisible because it is distributed — fifteen minutes here, an hour there, across every rep, every week.

### 4. Sales invents its own messaging

When the official answer is slow or unfindable, the field writes its own. Every rep becomes an unlicensed product marketer. Some improvisations are good; most drift. The company ends up with as many positioning statements as it has quota carriers, and the buyer — who talks to two reps, reads the website, and receives a proposal — sees three different stories from one vendor. Inconsistency reads as incoherence, and incoherence reads as risk.

### 5. New opportunities wait on a research sprint

A new vertical opens, a competitor stumbles, an analyst inquiry lands. Responding requires positioning, messaging, and assets for the new context — which, in the fragmented state, means a multi-week project starting from scratch. By the time the assets exist, the window has narrowed. Speed of response is a function of foundation freshness, and there is no foundation.

---

## What Inconsistency Actually Costs

The costs are real and mostly unbudgeted:

| Cost | Mechanism | Who feels it |
|------|-----------|--------------|
| Lost deals | Stale competitive claims walk reps into traps; inconsistent stories read as vendor risk to buying committees | Sales, CRO |
| Slower deals | Days-long turnaround for deal-specific assets; reps selling without proof points | Sales |
| Wasted selling time | Folder hunting and DIY asset creation are hours not spent with buyers | Sales, SDR |
| Proposal quality variance | RFP answers rewritten from memory each time, differently each time | Proposals |
| PMM capacity ceiling | One PMM saturates at 1–2 products; portfolio growth outpaces messaging coverage | Leadership |
| Brand erosion | Every off-foundation asset dilutes positioning a little; the sum is a fuzzy brand | Marketing, CEO |
| Onboarding drag | New reps learn messaging by inheriting whichever decks their pod uses | Sales enablement |

None of these appear on a budget line, which is why the problem persists. The queue is tolerated, the archaeology is tolerated, the drift is tolerated — until a lost deal review surfaces that two reps told the same buyer two different stories.

---

## Why the Obvious Fixes Fail

- **"Hire more PMMs."** Adds capacity, not consistency. Three PMMs with three mental models produce three canons. And the ratio still breaks at the next product launch.
- **"Put everything in a shared drive / wiki."** A repository solves storage, not retrieval, freshness, or framing. It becomes the archaeology site of failure mode 3.
- **"Buy a sales enablement platform."** Distributes content and measures usage — of whatever content is loaded, current or not, consistent or not. Garbage in, dashboards out. See [05 — Market Landscape](05-market-landscape.md).
- **"Just use an LLM."** A model with no validated foundation produces fluent, confident, generic output — the anti-generic mandate's (§8.1) definition of failure. Guessing at scale is still guessing.

Each fix addresses one failure mode and leaves the root untouched: **there is no single rigorous, current, queryable foundation that everything else traces to.**

---

## The Problem, Stated Once

> The company's product story lives in fragments — in the PMM's head, in stale decks, in scattered folders — so every team that needs it either waits in a queue, digs for it, or makes up its own. The fix is not more content or more people. It is one rigorous foundational doc per product, kept current, made queryable, and enforced as the source of every downstream answer and asset.

That fix is the product. [03 — Value Propositions](03-value-props.md) states what it is worth.
