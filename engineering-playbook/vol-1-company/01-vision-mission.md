From the day a road is designed to the day it is decommissioned, every inspection, every repair, every dollar spent, and every decision made will exist in a continuous, queryable, and trustworthy record. Capital investment decisions will be made with the same rigor that bond traders apply to risk — grounded in data, modeled against alternatives, and explainable to any stakeholder.

This is not a technology vision. It is an infrastructure stewardship vision. The technology is the means. The outcome is a world where public agencies can look any legislator in the eye and say: here is the condition of every asset we own, here is what it will cost to maintain it over the next decade, and here is the consequence of not funding it. Private owners will be able to show their boards precisely which assets threaten production targets and what investment is required to protect them.

When Aurigo fully realizes this vision, the question "where do we find that data?" will no longer be asked in infrastructure organizations. The data will exist, it will be current, and it will be connected to every decision that depends on it.

## The Problem We Are Solving

### Lifecycle Fragmentation: Data Dies When Projects Close

The infrastructure lifecycle has three distinct phases: Plan, Build, and Maintain. Every phase involves specialized software, specialized teams, and specialized workflows. The problem is not that these phases are separate. The problem is that the data generated in each phase rarely survives the transition to the next.

When a state DOT plans a bridge replacement project, engineers develop detailed condition assessments, structural models, design standards, and cost estimates. That information informs a capital program that may span five to ten years. When the project finally goes to construction, a construction management firm takes over. They use different software, a different data model, and a different team. The planning data lives in a capital program management system that the construction team does not use.

When construction completes and the bridge is commissioned, the maintenance organization inherits an asset about which they know surprisingly little. The as-built drawings are in a document management system. The material certifications are in a project files folder. The equipment warranties are in a spreadsheet. The precise condition of the bridge at the moment it opened to traffic — the baseline from which all future deterioration should be measured — is locked in a closeout report that no maintenance system can query.

The maintenance team opens their work order system and creates a new asset record. They enter what they can find. They estimate what they cannot. They start fresh. Twenty years of design intent, procurement history, inspection records, and construction data is effectively lost for operational purposes.

This is not a corner case. This is the standard state of affairs at virtually every infrastructure-owning organization in the United States.

### What This Costs

The cost of lifecycle fragmentation is not theoretical. It shows up in three places:

**Condition Assessment Redundancy.** Because maintenance organizations cannot trust the data they inherited from construction, they re-assess condition from scratch. Bridge inspectors conduct NBIS inspections on a two-year cycle. Pavement engineers run PCI surveys. The agencies doing this work already have better data from construction — they just cannot access it. The result is redundant inspection spend, often in the range of $50,000 to $500,000 per agency per year, depending on network size.

**Capital Planning Errors.** When capital plans are built on incomplete or stale condition data, the plans are wrong. Projects are funded too early (assets that could have lasted longer are replaced prematurely) or too late (assets that needed replacement are deferred until failure, which costs three to five times more than planned replacement). Federal infrastructure analysts estimate that suboptimal capital timing costs public agencies 20 to 30 percent in avoidable costs over a 20-year horizon.

**Compliance Risk.** Federal programs — FHWA's National Bridge Inspection Standards, the TAMP requirement for National Highway System assets, FAA's Pavement Management System requirements — mandate specific condition data, reporting formats, and planning methodologies. Agencies that lack lifecycle continuity struggle to produce compliant reports. Non-compliance risks federal funding eligibility.

## Why This Matters for Public Agencies

Public infrastructure agencies are stewards of public funds. They do not have the luxury of accepting risk the way a private investor does. When a county engineer proposes a bridge replacement at a cost of $8 million, that engineer will face questions from elected officials, taxpayers, and federal auditors. The questions will be: Why this bridge? Why now? Why $8 million? What happens if we wait five years?

These are not unreasonable questions. They are exactly the right questions. But without lifecycle data, they are nearly impossible to answer with evidence. The engineer answers from experience and judgment — which is valuable — but not from a defensible, auditable data trail.

The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA, 2021) represents $1.2 trillion in federal infrastructure investment over ten years. It includes specific requirements for asset management practices, TAMP compliance, and performance-based planning. Federal agencies are asking for the data to prove that money is being spent wisely. Masterworks Maintain is built to provide exactly that evidence base.

The TAMP requirement specifically mandates that state DOTs demonstrate:
- A complete inventory of NHS assets
- Condition assessments using standardized methods
- Performance gaps between current condition and targets
- A financial plan that demonstrates how the agency will close the gap
- Risk analysis of assets that are not funded

Agencies that can produce this evidence receive preferential treatment in federal discretionary grant programs. Agencies that cannot expose themselves to compliance findings and reduced funding. The stakes are real.

## Why This Matters for Private Owners

Private infrastructure owners face a different set of consequences, but the underlying problem is identical. When a semiconductor manufacturer's critical process equipment fails unexpectedly, the cost is not an abstract number. It is measurable in lost production hours, scrap materials, emergency repair premium, and customer delivery penalties. Industry estimates suggest that unplanned downtime in manufacturing costs between $50,000 and $500,000 per hour, depending on the process.

The lifecycle fragmentation problem for private owners manifests as: capital expenditure that does not align with actual asset condition, resulting in either premature replacement (waste) or reactive failure (crisis). The maintenance organization that manages aging production equipment frequently operates on experience and institutional knowledge rather than on a current, data-driven condition baseline. When the experienced maintenance supervisor retires, that institutional knowledge walks out the door.

Primus Maintain provides the institutional memory that private owners cannot afford to lose. It captures condition at commissioning, tracks it through inspection and maintenance activity, models deterioration, and produces a capital plan that finance can incorporate into annual and multi-year budgets with confidence.

For private owners, the business case is simple: every dollar spent on capital plan intelligence that prevents one day of unplanned downtime generates a return measured in multiples. For a data center operating at Tier III standards with an uptime SLA of 99.982%, a single unexpected generator failure during a utility outage is a SLA breach event with financial and reputational consequences. Knowing that a generator is in its fourth year of a ten-year lifecycle, due for a major overhaul, is the difference between planned maintenance cost and emergency replacement cost.

## Specific Infrastructure Failures Aurigo Prevents

Vision statements that do not name the failure mode they prevent are marketing copy. Aurigo exists to prevent five specific classes of infrastructure failure:

1. **Structurally deficient bridge closures.** In the U.S. today, roughly 42,400 of 617,000 bridges are classified structurally deficient (FHWA NBI, 2023). The average bridge closure due to unplanned condition failure diverts 5,700 vehicles per day and costs the local economy $8,000–$40,000 per day in detour productivity loss (TxDOT 2019 economic impact study). Maintain's condition tracking + deterioration models are designed to catch these bridges 3–5 years before failure so agencies can fund them into the STIP.
2. **Pavement decay past the 25% remaining-life cliff.** Once a pavement drops below a PCI of ~55, reconstruction cost is 4× the cost of a preservation treatment applied 3 years earlier (FHWA Pavement Preservation Effectiveness). Maintain's RUL engine surfaces the treatment window.
3. **Water main breaks in aging distribution networks.** AWWA reports 250,000+ main breaks per year in the U.S., costing $2.6B in direct repair and immeasurable secondary damage. Age + soil + pressure history + break history are the four predictors; Maintain captures all four.
4. **Unplanned generator/UPS failure in Tier III/IV data centers.** The Uptime Institute 2023 outage report attributes 45% of high-severity outages to power. A single Tier III SLA breach event averages $9,000 per minute of downtime. Maintain's private-sector deployment surfaces the maintenance-window signal before the failure event.
5. **FAA-mandated pavement condition non-compliance at Part 139 airports.** Airports that cannot produce a compliant PCI report risk AIP funding withhold. Maintain automates the PCI workflow to standards.

Every product decision is measured against: "does this help prevent one of these five failure modes, or improve our ability to prove that we did?"

## North Star Metric

**Aurigo's single North Star Metric is: Assets Under Continuous Lifecycle Management (AUCLM).**

An asset is counted in AUCLM if — for the trailing 90 days — it has (1) a canonical Aurigo asset ID, (2) at least one condition observation, (3) at least one authenticated user query against its record, and (4) is represented in a live capital plan scenario. This is a deliberately strict definition. It filters out "asset records" that exist in a database but are neither observed nor consulted — the exact failure state the vision was written to eliminate.

### Why this metric

AUCLM is the only metric that goes down if we sell a license and the customer does not adopt, and goes up if a customer expands from Plan-only to Plan+Maintain. It aligns sales, product, engineering, and CS on the same behavior: get more real assets into continuous, evidence-based stewardship. Revenue is a lagging indicator of AUCLM; AUCLM is a lagging indicator of product quality.

## How This Vision Constrains Product Decisions

The vision is not just aspirational. It is an active filter on the roadmap. Concretely:

- **If a proposed feature would create a data-loss point** (e.g., an export/import cycle without preserving lineage), it is rejected regardless of customer request volume.
- **If a proposed feature requires the customer to abandon their existing EAM to get value,** it fails the "integrate not replace" philosophy and is rejected or reworked into a Hybrid-mode design.
- **If a proposed feature cannot be explained in an audit trail** (AI decisions without provenance, black-box scoring), it does not ship to public-sector customers until the explainability requirement is met.
- **If a proposed feature does not raise AUCLM within 2 quarters of release,** it is pruned from the product.
