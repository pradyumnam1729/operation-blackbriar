# Engineering Director

## Mission

The Engineering Director owns the complete engineering function: delivery predictability, technical direction, team health, and the transition from a traditional software development organization to an AI-native engineering organization. In the Aurigo Maintain context, the ED is the person ultimately accountable for every line of code that ships — whether that code was written by a human engineer, generated by Claude, or assembled from a combination of both.

The most important mindset shift for an Engineering Director in an AI-native organization is this: your job is no longer to ensure that individual engineers write correct code. Your job is to ensure that the system of humans and AI agents produces correct code consistently and at scale. The leverage is fundamentally different.

---

## Responsibilities

### Engineering Execution

Own the delivery of every sprint commitment. Run weekly sprint reviews with the team, reviewing velocity trends, identifying blockers, and making the calls required to keep commitments realistic. The ED does not blame the team for missed sprints — the ED either fixed the impediment, descoped the sprint, or absorbed the slip with a clear explanation.

Maintain a 12-week rolling delivery forecast, updated weekly. Product and leadership should never be surprised by a slipped delivery date; they should see it coming 4+ weeks in advance with a clear explanation and mitigation plan.

### Technical Direction

Make or ratify every significant technical decision. In practice, this means reviewing and approving ADRs (Architecture Decision Records) written by the Tech Architect and Backend/Frontend Leads. The ED ensures that technical decisions align with the strategic direction (Clean Architecture, AI-first, federal compliance) and don't create contradictions or technical debt that will slow the team down.

Enforce Clean Architecture boundaries. In an AI-native team where code generation happens rapidly, boundary violations (infrastructure concerns leaking into domain logic, business rules in controllers) accumulate faster than in a traditional team. The ED is accountable for ensuring the automated architecture review gate actually catches these violations and that engineers resolve them before merge.

### AI Agent Strategy

Design and evolve the team's AI agent workflow. This means: deciding which tasks are AI-primary (code generation, test generation, documentation drafting), which tasks are human-primary (ADR writing, domain logic review, security review), and which tasks are AI-assisted human-executed (PR review, incident response).

Track the AI agent productivity multiplier: the ratio of features delivered per engineer-sprint-week compared to the pre-AI baseline. This is not a vanity metric — it is the core business case for the AI-native investment. If the multiplier is not improving quarter-over-quarter, diagnose why and make changes.

### Team Health

In an AI-native org, team health requires specific attention to the human side of a changing role. Engineers who thrived as individual code writers may struggle when their primary job becomes reviewing and directing AI-generated code. The ED identifies these transitions early and supports engineers in developing new skills: architectural reasoning, prompt engineering, quality judgment, domain knowledge acquisition.

Conduct weekly 1:1s with each direct report. The primary agenda item: what is blocking you, and what do you need from me?

### Knowledge Institutionalization

An AI-native organization is at specific risk of generating undocumented decisions. When Claude generates a migration strategy or a data model design in a conversation, that reasoning needs to be captured in an ADR or a playbook update — or it is lost. The ED enforces a culture where every significant decision produces a durable artifact.

Run a monthly "knowledge capture" session where the team reviews the last month's decisions and ensures every consequential one is documented in the vault.

## Authority

The ED has final authority on:
- Technical architecture decisions (overrides Tech Architect if necessary)
- Team structure and role assignments
- Build/buy/integrate decisions for infrastructure components
- Escalation of critical technical debt requiring sprint capacity reprioritization
- Performance management of all engineering roles

The ED does not have unilateral authority on:
- Product scope (requires Product Manager agreement)
- Hiring budget (requires Director-level approval)
- Production deployments without QA Lead sign-off
- Security architecture changes without explicit security review

**Weekly**: Sprint velocity report, blocker log, AI agent productivity notes

**Monthly**: Engineering health dashboard (DORA metrics, test coverage, AI multiplier), knowledge capture session output, technical debt inventory

**Quarterly**: Engineering roadmap update, AI agent strategy review, team skill development assessment

**Annually**: Year-in-review engineering report, technology evaluation (should any stack choices be reconsidered?), team org design review

## Decision Making

The ED uses a documented decision framework for major technical choices:

1. **Reversibility assessment**: Is this decision easily reversible, or does it create lock-in? Reversible decisions are made quickly; irreversible decisions get ADR treatment.
2. **Alignment check**: Does this decision align with the existing ADR corpus and the playbook? If it contradicts an existing ADR, that ADR must be explicitly superseded.
3. **AI-agent compatibility**: Can an AI agent operate within the constraints of this decision? If a technical decision requires so much implicit context that an AI agent can't follow it, the decision needs better documentation.
4. **Cost of delay**: What is the cost of delaying this decision? Many architectural decisions get better with more information; others (missing migrations, accumulating technical debt) get worse.

## Daily Workflow

**08:00–08:30** — Review overnight CI results, deployment status, and any production alerts. Triage anything that needs immediate attention.

**08:30–09:00** — Review AI-generated code PRs. Not line-by-line review — pattern recognition. Is the code organized as expected? Are there architectural anomalies that suggest the AI misunderstood a constraint? Flag for deeper review by the relevant lead.

**09:00–10:30** — Meetings: standups (attend 2 per week to stay close to the work), 1:1s (2 per day on average), stakeholder syncs.

**10:30–12:00** — Strategic work: ADR reviews, playbook updates, roadmap thinking, AI agent strategy evolution.

**13:00–15:00** — Unblocking: focused time for resolving the blockers identified in the morning. Pair with engineers, make decisions, draft architectural guidance.

**15:00–17:00** — Administrative: sprint planning prep, metrics review, hiring, documentation.

---

## Collaboration

**With Product Manager**: Weekly product/engineering sync to review backlog priority, upcoming sprint scope, and trade-off decisions. The ED ensures engineering constraints are visible to the PM before scope commitments are made to customers.

**With Tech Architect**: Daily alignment on architecture decisions. The ED delegates architectural design to the Architect; the ED reviews and approves. When they disagree, the ED has final call but documents the reasoning.

**With QA Lead**: The ED supports the QA Lead's authority to block a release. The ED resolves the underlying quality issue; the QA Lead controls the gate.

**With Lifecycle Domain Expert**: Monthly alignment on domain accuracy. If the domain expert flags that a calculation or workflow doesn't reflect real infrastructure operations, the ED prioritizes the correction.

---

## Escalation

The ED escalates to the VP of Engineering when:
- A delivery commitment to a customer is at risk by > 2 weeks
- A critical security vulnerability is found in a production deployment
- An engineer performance issue requires HR involvement
- The AI agent productivity multiplier is declining for 2+ consecutive quarters

The ED absorbs escalations from the team when:
- A significant technical decision is blocked by disagreement between roles
- A customer request requires a scope exception that would impact delivery
- A production incident requires cross-team coordination

---

## Continuous Improvement

The ED drives a quarterly retrospective that specifically examines AI agent effectiveness:
- Which agent workflows produced the highest quality code?
- Which agent outputs required the most human rework?
- Are the ADRs and playbook documents sufficiently clear to guide AI-generated code?
- Are there new AI capabilities (model versions, tools) that should be incorporated?

The goal is a quarterly improvement in the AI agent productivity multiplier through better prompts, better playbook documentation, better constraint specification, and better review workflows.

## Example Scenarios

### Scenario 1: AI-Generated Migration Violates Multi-Tenancy

During a PR review, the ED notices that an AI-generated EF Core migration adds a new table but the corresponding query handler doesn't apply the tenant_id filter. The AI correctly implemented the feature functionality but missed the cross-cutting concern.

The ED's response: (1) blocks the PR with a clear comment explaining the violation, (2) checks whether the automated architecture review gate should have caught this (and files a ticket to improve the gate if it didn't), (3) schedules a 30-minute team discussion on multi-tenancy patterns to ensure everyone — and the AI prompts — carry the constraint explicitly.

This is not a blame event. It is a system improvement event. The goal is to ensure this class of violation is caught automatically next time.

### Scenario 2: Sprint Velocity Drops 40% for Two Consecutive Sprints

The metrics dashboard shows two consecutive sprints significantly under the rolling average velocity. The ED investigates: Are stories being underestimated? Is there a technical blocker that is consuming unplanned capacity? Is team morale affecting productivity?

After 1:1s, the ED discovers that the AI agent workflow is producing code that engineers don't trust — they're rewriting AI-generated code from scratch rather than reviewing and editing it. The ED convenes a team session, examines specific examples of AI code that engineers rejected, and identifies that the system prompt for the code generation agent lacks specificity about the EF Core patterns used in this codebase.

The ED works with the Tech Architect to improve the system prompt, documents the patterns more clearly in the playbook, and tracks whether the next two sprints show improvement. They do.

### Scenario 3: Managing the Transition to AI-Native for a Senior Engineer

A senior backend engineer, a strong individual contributor, is struggling in the AI-native model. They spend twice as long reviewing AI-generated code as the other engineers, partly because they don't trust it and partly because their mental model of "doing software engineering" is still centered on writing code.

The ED recognizes this early in the 1:1 cadence, before it becomes a performance issue. They have a direct conversation: the role is changing, and the engineer needs to develop new skills. Together they identify three specific areas: (1) learning to recognize architectural patterns quickly in code they didn't write, (2) developing prompting skills that produce higher-quality initial output, (3) accepting that perfect code generated in 10 minutes is better than perfect code written in 3 hours.

The ED pairs the engineer with the AI Engineer for a 4-week mentorship on prompting and AI-supervised review techniques. Within 2 months, the engineer's review throughput is on par with the team.

## Hiring Criteria

### Background

The Engineering Director role at Aurigo requires a specific blend of experience: enough hands-on engineering to earn credibility with the team, enough people leadership to run a distributed team effectively, and enough strategic thinking to shape the AI-native operating model.

**Required background**:
- 10+ years of software engineering experience, at least 5 of which include leadership (Manager, Director, Head of Engineering)
- Direct experience shipping enterprise SaaS to regulated customers (public sector, healthcare, financial services)
- Experience with .NET and modern web stacks (React/TypeScript preferred but not required — must be able to learn quickly)
- Experience running an on-call rotation and incident response process
- Demonstrated experience adopting AI tools (Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code) in a team setting, not just personally

**Highly desirable**:
- Experience with EAM, ERP, or asset management software domain
- Public sector customer experience (understands procurement cycles, TAMP, federal compliance)
- Startup or scaleup experience (comfortable in ambiguity, not attached to bureaucracy)
- Experience with distributed engineering teams

**Deal-breakers**:
- "Command and control" leadership style (does not scale in AI-native ops)
- Discomfort with rapid change (AI stack evolves quarterly)
- Cannot articulate their own experience shipping code recently (out of touch = out of credibility)

### Interview Process

1. **Recruiter screen (30 min)**: motivation, salary alignment, availability, high-level career fit
2. **CTO / VP Engineering screen (60 min)**: leadership philosophy, deep dive on one specific outcome they drove, AI-native operating model perspective
3. **Technical depth interview (75 min)**: system design case (design an integration between Maintain and a legacy EAM, at whiteboard level), followed by a code review of a real AI-generated PR from our codebase (evaluate quality of feedback)
4. **Team panel (60 min)**: 4 engineers from different roles ask questions. Panel evaluates: would we trust this person to make judgment calls that affect our work?
5. **Culture and values (45 min)**: HR/People + one exec. Focus on: how they handle disagreement, how they handle failure, how they attribute credit
6. **References**: 3 required, at least 1 direct report, at least 1 peer, at least 1 manager

**Total time**: ~5-6 hours across 2-3 weeks

### Signals to Look For

- Speaks about "the team" and "we" more than "I"
- Can describe a specific incident postmortem they ran and what they learned
- Has fired an underperforming engineer and can explain how they knew and how they handled it
- Has an explicit view on AI-native engineering (not just "AI is exciting" — has thought about how it changes the operating model)
- Willing to disagree with the interviewer during the interview (courage is a required trait)

### Red Flags

- Cannot describe recent code they've read or written
- Every problem is described as "someone else's fault"
- Uncomfortable with the phrase "I don't know" — always has an answer
- Describes "AI adoption" as a personal productivity hack, not a team operating change
- Cannot name a specific mistake they made and what they learned

## Onboarding Timeline

### Day 1

- Welcome + laptop + AWS SSO + GitHub Admin access + Claude Code + PagerDuty + Slack admin
- Meet with the CTO / VP Engineering for a 90-minute context download: current state of engineering, top 3 concerns, top 3 initiatives, exec team dynamics
- Meet with the PM for 60 minutes: product state, customer commitments, top escalations
- Read Volumes 1, 2, and 4 of the Engineering Playbook (the operating model documents)
- Attend the daily standup as an observer

**Success signal for Day 1**: can articulate what Aurigo Maintain is, who the top 3 customers are, and what the sprint goal is

### Week 1

- 1:1 with every direct report (Backend Lead, Frontend Lead, QA Lead, DevOps, Tech Architect if separate)
- 1:1 with every peer stakeholder (PM, VP Sales, VP Customer Success, Head of Design)
- Read Volumes 3, 5, and 6 (architecture, operating model, integrations)
- Read the last 3 months of incident postmortems
- Read the current backlog top-20 stories
- Attend one sprint planning, one sprint review, one retrospective (across multiple weeks)
- Do NOT make any decisions in Week 1. Observe, listen, document.

**Success signal for Week 1**: has a written list of "things I've observed" and "questions I need to answer" — no premature action

### Month 1

- Complete the "listening tour": every direct report has had 3+ 1:1s
- Attend a customer call with the PM
- Attend an incident (or observe an incident postmortem)
- Own one PR review completely (understand our patterns from the ground up)
- Publish a "Day 30 observations" doc: what's working, what's not, what needs immediate attention (do not announce major changes yet)
- Establish the ED metrics dashboard and start tracking

**Success signal for Month 1**: the team feels heard. Direct reports say "the ED listens" not "the ED has all the answers already."

### Month 3

- Publish the "Day 90 plan": specific initiatives, prioritized, with owners and success criteria
- Have made at least 2 significant decisions with rationale documented in ADRs or playbook updates
- Have hired or promoted at least one person (if the team needs it)
- Established weekly all-hands cadence with predictable format
- Have run at least one full incident as Incident Commander

**Success signal for Month 3**: measurable improvement in one core metric (velocity, MTTR, defect escape rate, or engineer satisfaction). The team says "the ED made this better" for something specific.

## Performance Review Criteria (for the ED role)

The ED is reviewed by the CTO / VP Engineering on:

**1. Engineering outcome delivery (40%)**
- Are commitments met?
- Are DORA metrics improving?
- Are incidents decreasing?
- Are the top customer escalations resolved?

**2. Team health (25%)**
- Retention rate
- Engineer satisfaction score (quarterly survey)
- Are people growing? (promotions, expanded scope)
- Are 1:1s happening?

**3. AI-native operating model maturity (20%)**
- Is the AI multiplier trending upward?
- Are agent constraint documents evolving with observed patterns?
- Is the team routinely using AI for the workflows described in the playbook?

**4. Strategic leadership (15%)**
- Is the engineering roadmap aligned with product strategy?
- Are the right investments being made (build vs buy, tech debt vs feature)?
- Is the ED a peer to the PM, VP Sales, etc. — trusted, credible, present?

The ED is not evaluated on lines of code, personal PRs, or individual technical contributions.
