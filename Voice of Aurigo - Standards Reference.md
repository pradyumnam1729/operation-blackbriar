THE CARDINAL RULE

Every piece of Aurigo content, regardless of format or audience, must open from the reader's world — their decision point, their constraint, their risk, or their situation right now. Not from Aurigo. Not from the product. Not from context-setting about the industry.

This is the most important rule. Everything else follows from it.

THE SIX CONSTANTS

Apply across all three voice modes. Never change regardless of surface, channel, or audience.

Be this	Not this
Conversational — write like a trusted advisor who knows the room	Corporate — write like a brochure
Knowledgeable — show domain command. Capital programs, CIP, right of way, RFI, change orders.	Jargon-heavy — dropping technical terms to sound expert
Confident — make direct claims about what Aurigo does	Hedged — "may," "could," "might," "potentially"
Direct — short sentences, active voice, clear subject	Passive — buried leads, roundabout constructions
Reader-first — open from their decision, their constraint, their world	Product-first — open from the company or the feature
Precise — name the specific outcome: on-time delivery, audit readiness, budget adherence	Vague — "better outcomes," "greater efficiency," "maximize value"

USAGE AND WORD CHOICE

Rules for specific words, constructions, and terms. Apply before any content is approved.

Public vs. Commercial Sector Terminology

Program vs. portfolio

Use program in public-sector and government contexts (capital program, infrastructure program, CIP).
Use portfolio in commercial/facility-owner contexts (capital portfolio, investment portfolio).
Do not swap them. A state DOT manages a program. A data center VP manages a portfolio.
Flag if: "portfolio" appears in Masterworks or Essentials content; "program" used in Primus/commercial content.

Government agencies, not public agencies

Use "government agencies" — precise and how these organizations identify.
"Public agencies" is vague and less accurate. Do not use it.

Local and regional agencies, not small and medium

"Small and medium" is a size classification, not how government buyers describe themselves.
Use "local and regional agencies" in customer-facing content.

Audience Hierarchy — CEO confirmed June 2026

Capital owners is the umbrella term covering all segments. It splits into:

Public owners (= government agencies): Federal + State & Local Government. Served by Masterworks and Essentials.
Facility owners: Commercial sector — data centers, manufacturing, life sciences, energy and utilities. Served by Primus.
Term	Scope	Use in content
Capital owners	All segments — umbrella	Use when addressing all audiences together. Never use for a single segment alone.
Public owners (= Government agencies)	Government sector — federal and state/local government	Use for public-sector content. Palantir equivalent: "Government."
Federal agencies	Sub-segment of public owners: USDOT, FHWA, DOTs, MPOs	Use on federal pages and USDOT BPA context. Most specific public-sector term.
State & local government (SLG)	Sub-segment of public owners: DOTs, cities, counties, MPOs	"Local and regional agencies" for Essentials buyers specifically.
Facility owners	Commercial sector — private capital program owners	Use for Primus content only. Palantir equivalent: "Commercial." Do NOT use in Masterworks or Essentials content.
Infrastructure owners	RETIRED — do not use	Ambiguous and inconsistent. Replace with "capital owners," "public owners," or "facility owners" as appropriate.

ROI

Appropriate in commercial/Primus content.
Do not use in government or public-sector content.
Use "program outcomes" or "capital program performance" instead.
Product and Technical Terminology

Life cycle — two words, always

"life cycle," never "lifecycle."
Applies to: capital life cycle, asset life cycle, project life cycle.

Right of way — no hyphens in standalone use

"right of way" as a standalone noun.
"right-of-way" hyphenated is acceptable only as an attributive adjective directly before a noun ("right-of-way management" is acceptable; "the right-of-way" is not).

Infrastructure — uncountable noun, never pluralized

Never "infrastructures."
Correct: "infrastructure projects" / "infrastructure programs" / "capital infrastructure."
Avoid "private infrastructure" — use "other infrastructure" or name the specific type.

Contingency, cash flow, validation — do not pluralize

Correct: "contingency planning," "cash flow forecasting," "validation process."
Incorrect: "contingencies" (financial modeling context), "cash flows" (as a heading), "validations."

Single source of truth — avoid

Overused across the industry. Use "unified system" instead.
Only in a BUILD (delivery/execution) context, not in a PLAN (planning/capital) context.

On time, on budget — avoid as a standalone claim

Reads as a cliché. Use instead: "keep projects on track" / "deliver within budget and on schedule" / "aligned to plan."

Paper-based audit trail

Always "paper-based audit trail," not "paper trail" or "papertrail."
"Paper trail" carries a negative investigative connotation.

In the field / in the cloud

"In the field" — not "on the field."
"In the cloud" — not "on the cloud."
Naming Conventions

Organization abbreviations — no "the"

Never precede an organization abbreviation with "the."
Correct: "NDOT manages..." / "FHWA requires..."
Incorrect: "The NDOT manages..." / "The FHWA requires..."

Clients vs. End Users (public sector)

In public-sector contexts, the people who use the system downstream are "end users," not "clients."
"Clients" implies a fee-for-service relationship. In government contexts, the public are constituents, not clients.

"Such as" vs. "like"

Use "such as" when introducing examples in formal and Enterprise Voice content.
"Like" is acceptable in conversational Human Voice contexts only.
Writing Patterns to Avoid

Binary contrast framing ("This is not X. It is Y.")

Occasional use is effective. Overuse signals AI-generated content.
Maximum once per piece. More than one is a hard violation.

Em dash — strict limit

Useful for sharp breaks but overuse makes prose feel fragmented and AI-generated.
Maximum one to two per page in external content.
Prefer a period or a comma when either works equally well.

"Hassle-free" / "effortlessly"

Too salesy. Remove.
Describe what is actually easy, or let the simplicity speak through the description.

"Defensible" — do not use in government-sector content

Carries a defensive or legal connotation inappropriate for government-facing materials.
Use "transparent and accountable program delivery" instead.
AI MESSAGING RULES — CEO confirmed June 2026

"AI-native" is the only approved modifier for Aurigo products. Do not use: AI-powered / AI-driven / AI-enabled / AI-based / AI/ML. Exception: meta descriptions and page-level SEO fields may use "AI-powered" for search ranking only. Not in body copy.

Lumina framing:

Position Lumina as the foundational engine, not a standalone product.
Approved: "Our copilots and agents run on Lumina." / "Masterworks AI, powered by Lumina."
"Masterworks AI, powered by Lumina" is the ONLY approved "powered by" construction. Remove "powered by" language everywhere else.
Aurigo owns three layers of the AI stack: the Lumina data model, the Masterworks system of record, and the agents.

Agent language:

Name the agent type specifically: Prediction Agent, Execution Agent. Do not use "Agent" generically.
Copilot, Predict, and Execute are individual products within Masterworks AI / Primus AI.

Public sector AI messaging:

Approved message: "do more with the same people." Same team, far greater program capacity.
Never frame AI as headcount reduction in government content.
Never use "ROI" in government or public-sector copy. Use "program outcomes" or "capital program performance."

Deterministic vs. inference framing (use in FAQs, thought leadership, "How AI works" content):

60% of what a capital program team does is deterministic. Agents handle that.
The remaining 40% is inference — judgement calls — and humans stay in the loop.

Content structure:

Lead with What and Why, not How. The How lives behind "How it Works" or "Learn more."
Answer "What does this do for me?" before introducing technology or features.
Apply the company-name-swap test: replace "Aurigo" with "Oracle," "Microsoft," or "Kahua." If the content still reads, rewrite it. Every claim must be irreplaceably Aurigo for that specific audience.
Every page explains three things: what the software does, the industry challenge it solves, and the customer outcome — specific, named, and measurable.
FIVE TESTS BEFORE PUBLISHING

Run these on every piece before it goes out.

Reader test. Does the first line open from the reader's world — their challenge, constraint, or decision? Not from Aurigo?
Specificity test. Are all claims backed by a number, a named product, a named audience, or a concrete outcome? If a sentence could describe any enterprise software company, rewrite it.
Banned phrase test. Does any line contain a word from the banned list?
Active voice test. Does every sentence have a clear subject doing the action? Find passive constructions and flip them.
AI visibility test. Could an LLM pull a clean, accurate, standalone summary from this page? Are key facts structured, named, and citable?
Swap test. Replace "Aurigo" with a competitor name (Oracle, Microsoft, Kahua). If the sentence still works, the claim is not specific enough. Rewrite until it is irreplaceably Aurigo.
